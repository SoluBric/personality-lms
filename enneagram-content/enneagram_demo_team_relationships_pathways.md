# Enneagram Demo LMS — Team Relationships and Team Development Pathways

**Status:** Working demo content architecture  
**Scope:** Seven actual pair relationships, two team-level profiles, team development themes, team learning pathways, viewer-specific interaction guidance, and selective reuse of the existing 22-course catalogue.  
**Purpose:** Add the social/team layer to the demo without expanding into every possible Enneagram pairing.

---

# 1. Team-Layer Design Principle

The team layer should not expose every detail of another person's personality profile.

Instead, the purpose is to help each learner answer practical questions:

- How might this colleague naturally approach work differently from me?
- What strengths do we bring to one another?
- Where might misunderstanding or friction occur?
- What might I misread about their behaviour?
- What can I do differently?
- What can we practise together?
- Which shared courses or pathways would help us work more effectively?

The team layer therefore uses selected personality information to create **relationship guidance**, not psychological surveillance.

The demo contains:

- **Team Atlas:** Emily Carter, James Bennett, Sophie Harris, Thomas Parker
- **Team Beacon:** Sarah Mitchell, Oliver Grant

There are only **seven actual pair relationships**:

### Team Atlas
1. Emily ↔ James
2. Emily ↔ Sophie
3. Emily ↔ Thomas
4. James ↔ Sophie
5. James ↔ Thomas
6. Sophie ↔ Thomas

### Team Beacon
7. Sarah ↔ Oliver

---

# 2. Relationship Content Model

Each pair relationship uses the same compact structure:

- **Natural complementarity**
- **Likely misunderstanding**
- **Potential friction**
- **What Person A can learn from Person B**
- **What Person B can learn from Person A**
- **What each person should keep in mind**
- **Recommended joint development theme**
- **Relevant shared courses**

The user-facing application could show the same relationship differently depending on who is viewing it.

For example:

> **Emily viewing James**  
> “His directness may be intended as efficiency rather than hostility.”

versus:

> **James viewing Emily**  
> “Her initial agreement may not mean she has fully committed to the decision.”

This is a stronger demo than giving both users an identical generic compatibility paragraph.

# 3. Team Atlas Pair Relationships

# 3.1 Emily Carter ↔ James Bennett
**Emily:** 9 / 2 / 6  
**James:** 8 / 3 / 1

## Natural complementarity

Emily brings:

- listening;
- team cohesion;
- mediation;
- interpersonal sensitivity;
- careful consideration of how decisions affect others.

James brings:

- decisiveness;
- momentum;
- willingness to confront difficult issues;
- accountability;
- strong standards.

Together, they can create a useful balance between **inclusion and action**.

Emily can help James notice relational information and concerns that urgency may otherwise bypass.

James can help Emily move from synthesis and accommodation toward visible decisions and action.

## Likely misunderstanding

Emily may experience James's directness as unnecessarily confrontational or as closing discussion too quickly.

James may experience Emily's calmness or apparent agreement as confirmation that the issue has been resolved.

The central misunderstanding is:

> **James may think the decision is settled before Emily has actually expressed her disagreement.**

## Potential friction

- James drives closure while Emily still sees unresolved interpersonal concerns.
- Emily delays voicing disagreement.
- James becomes more forceful because he interprets hesitation as lack of ownership.
- Emily withdraws further because increased force confirms that disagreement feels unsafe.
- Both then reinforce the other's least useful pattern.

## What Emily can learn from James

- disagreement does not automatically damage the relationship;
- stating a position early can prevent larger conflict later;
- action can create information;
- leadership sometimes requires choosing before everyone is comfortable.

## What James can learn from Emily

- silence is not always consent;
- relational information is operational information;
- decisions can fail because people were never genuinely committed;
- creating space for dissent can increase rather than reduce authority.

## Emily should keep in mind

James's directness is often an attempt to create clarity and momentum rather than a desire for interpersonal conflict.

## James should keep in mind

Emily may need explicit invitation and sufficient space to disagree before her real position becomes visible.

## Joint development theme
### **Productive disagreement without avoidance or domination**

## Shared courses
- **C06 — Constructive Conflict**
- **C08 — Listening Before Leading**
- **C20 — Facilitating Productive Disagreement**
- **C22 — High-Trust Team Decisions**

## Emily should keep in mind

Sophie's challenge may be directed at the quality or meaning of the work rather than at the relationship.

## Sophie should keep in mind

# 4.1 Sarah Mitchell ↔ Oliver Grant
**Sarah:** 9 / 5 / 3  
**Oliver:** 3 / 7 / 8

## Natural complementarity

Oliver brings energy, opportunity recognition, persuasion and movement.

Sarah brings synthesis, analysis, steadiness and pragmatic execution.

Both have strong Type 3 influence, which gives them a shared appreciation for results.

The pairing works particularly well when:

- Oliver opens opportunities;
- Sarah evaluates and structures them;
- Oliver creates momentum;
- Sarah protects delivery credibility;
- both then commit to a visible result.

## Likely misunderstanding

Oliver may interpret Sarah's caution or quiet analysis as unnecessary slowing.

Sarah may interpret Oliver's rapid commitment as insufficiently grounded in capacity or risk.

## Potential friction

- Oliver sees an opportunity and begins creating commitment.
- Sarah has reservations but initially keeps them private.
- momentum increases before her concerns are visible.
- Sarah eventually has to constrain an expectation that Oliver already considers active.

This can create the recurring dynamic:

> **Oliver believes Sarah is applying the brakes too late; Sarah believes Oliver accelerated before the road was checked.**

## What Sarah can learn from Oliver

- earlier visible action can create useful information;
- opportunity sometimes requires commitment before certainty;
- influence grows when thinking is communicated before it is complete.

## What Oliver can learn from Sarah

- credibility depends on capacity as well as enthusiasm;
- a thoughtful pause can strengthen rather than kill an opportunity;
- explicit constraints improve the quality of commitments.

## Sarah should keep in mind

Oliver's speed is often a genuine strength; the goal is not to slow him universally but to make decision gates visible.

## Oliver should keep in mind

Sarah's concerns are most useful before a promise is made, so he must actively invite them early.

# 5. Team Atlas — Team-Level Profile

## Team composition

- **Emily Carter:** 9 / 2 / 6 — relationship, cohesion, support, preparedness
- **James Bennett:** 8 / 3 / 1 — decisive leadership, delivery, standards
- **Sophie Harris:** 4 / 7 / 1 — originality, possibility, quality
- **Thomas Parker:** 5 / 6 / 7 — analysis, risk, scenarios

## Collective strengths

Team Atlas has unusually strong **complementarity of function**.

It contains:

- relational awareness;
- decisive action;
- creative exploration;
- analytical depth;
- quality orientation;
- risk awareness;
- strong delivery energy.

No single way of operating dominates all four members.

That means the team's potential is high because different members naturally notice different categories of information.

## Collective risks

The same diversity creates predictable coordination risks:

### 1. Speed versus deliberation
James may want closure while Thomas wants further analysis and Sophie wants further exploration.

### 2. Harmony versus productive disagreement
Emily may reduce visible conflict at precisely the point the team needs differences to become explicit.

### 3. Exploration versus completion
Sophie and Thomas can expand the problem from different directions while James pushes toward closure.

### 4. Unequal airtime
James's confidence and speed can unintentionally dominate a group containing two people who are more likely to process before speaking.

### 5. Hidden disagreement
A meeting can appear more aligned than it actually is.

# 7. Team Atlas Shared Learning Pathway

## Pathway: **From Diverse Perspectives to Confident Decisions**

### Stage 1 — Hear the Differences
**C08 — Listening Before Leading**

Purpose:
Create explicit space for reservations, alternative ideas and analytical concerns before the team moves toward closure.

### Stage 2 — Surface the Conflict
**C06 — Constructive Conflict**

Purpose:
Normalise disagreement as useful information rather than a sign that the team is failing to align.

### Stage 3 — Decide Without Perfect Certainty
**C10 — Decisions Without Certainty**

Purpose:
Give the team a common language for deciding when there is enough information to act.

### Stage 4 — Integrate the Room
**C20 — Facilitating Productive Disagreement**

Purpose:
Help the team prevent either domination or avoidance while synthesising different perspectives.

### Stage 5 — Commit Together
**C22 — High-Trust Team Decisions**

Purpose:
Create a repeatable decision process in which dissent is visible before closure and commitment becomes explicit afterward.

### Optional side branch — Creative Delivery
**C14 — From Idea to Delivery**  
→ **C15 — Feedback Without Losing the Work**

This branch is particularly relevant to Sophie and James but can be completed by the whole team.

# 8. Team Atlas Viewer-Specific Examples

A powerful demo feature is that each member can see the same team problem through a personalised lens.

## Team issue:
**“We are taking too long to make project decisions.”**

### Emily sees:
> You may be keeping options open because unresolved concerns have not been voiced. Your contribution is to help the group surface disagreement—but also to state your own position rather than only facilitating everyone else's.

### James sees:
> Your drive for closure is useful. The risk is that increased pressure can reduce the amount of disagreement you hear. Ask for opposing views before announcing the preferred decision.

### Sophie sees:
> Continued exploration can improve the solution, but the team needs an explicit point at which possibilities become an experiment rather than another round of ideation.

### Thomas sees:
> Your analysis improves decision quality. The development challenge is to define what information would materially change the decision and communicate a recommendation before every uncertainty is resolved.

This is the same team issue with four different behavioural entry points.

# 14. Team Recommendation Metadata

Team recommendations should support metadata similar to individual recommendations.

```text
TeamRecommendation
- team_id
- course_id
- priority
- shared_development_theme
- reason
- contributing_relationships[]
- contributing_team_patterns[]
- pathway_id
- pathway_order
```

A pair-specific recommendation can contain:

```text
RelationshipRecommendation
- viewer_id
- colleague_id
- course_id
- relationship_theme
- viewer_specific_reason
- what_to_notice
- suggested_behaviour
```

This allows the same pair to generate different guidance depending on the viewer.

# 15. What Another Team Member Can See

For the demo, the default team view could expose:

- colleague name and role;
- primary type;
- perhaps secondary and tertiary type as a simplified profile;
- key working strengths;
- preferred contribution style;
- likely friction points;
- “working well with me” guidance;
- relevant shared courses.

It should **not** expose by default:

- the colleague's full nine-score distribution;
- private stress details;
- personal blind-spot notes written for the individual;
- sensitive self-reflection responses;
- psychological “health level.”

This distinction makes the team feature feel professional rather than invasive.

# 20. Recommended Next Phase

The core **content architecture is now largely complete**.

The next useful work should shift from inventing content toward defining how the content is represented and navigated.

A sensible next phase would be:

1. define the **demo data/content schema** more formally;
2. define the main pages/screens and what content appears on each;
3. map the user journey from assessment result → profile → development map → course → team;
4. decide how skills, courses and pathways are visualised;
5. identify where the subtle RPG / forging / constellation ideas add value;
6. determine which pieces of demo content actually need to be visible in the first prototype and which can remain behind the scenes.

There is no strong reason to add more personality theory, more courses or more team pairings before that work begins.
